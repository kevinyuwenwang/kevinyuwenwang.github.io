About

I build business and systems in New York. The through-line across everything I work on is finding the small, correct mechanism that moves a whole thing—whether it's a trading model, a control panel, or a team of two. I'm not the loudest in the room and I don't need to be. The work either holds or it doesn't, and I'd rather know which one it is before I show anyone.

This page is the short version. The longer one takes a while.

The Story

Shanghai, middle school. I was the kid in the principal's office most Thursdays. Half the time for disruption. The other half for whatever side hustle I was running—by 13 I was selling jailbroken iPhone apps to classmates and making more than most people my age earned in a year. At 15 my parents decided I needed a different zip code. Denver suburbs. A small academy where nobody knew my name and I didn't know anyone's.

First year was mostly silence. I'd learned English from textbooks, and the social architecture of a school where everyone'd been in the same youth group since third grade was a language I simply didn't speak. Junior year I decided I could fix the gap with effort alone. No talent, no head start—just hours. Basketball, soccer, running after school until my legs gave out. A steady diet of Jimmy John's. Lost 30 kilos in a year. And the thing that actually changed wasn't the weight. It was that I'd proven to myself a boring, unsexy thing: sustained effort compounds, no matter what you start from. That belief got me into the classroom. Student government treasurer. An interest in finance I couldn't quite name yet.

College is where the interest got a name and a price tag. I opened a brokerage account at 18 and went through the phases: dividends first—Macy's stock, just to feel like I was getting paid for holding. Then Bitcoin, because I got impatient. Then a month of YouTube gurus and hours of staring at charts for patterns that weren't there. Then leverage. 10x, 20x. For a stretch it felt like the market was just handing me money. Then a Tuesday whipsaw wiped the whole account. I got the margin call mid-trip, on a packed itinerary. Didn't go to the hotel. Slept in the car. A few months later I tried options. 100x leverage. Gone faster than the rest.

I don't think I was stupid. I think I was optimizing for the wrong variable—chasing the next win instead of understanding the mechanism underneath. The distinction only became visible to me on a junior-year afternoon when I picked up Optimal Mean Reversion Trading. The equations were a foreign language. But the structure was clear: this tier of math is the line between a trader and a gambler, and I'd been gambling. My foundation was too thin for what I was attempting. So I added a mathematics minor halfway through. My parents thought I'd lost my mind.

Graduate school made the career question unavoidable. Watching classmates grind for the same three finance internships, and knowing I'd always thought better alone than at a networking event, I started looking sideways. Then LLMs arrived and the whole map shifted. Months of late-night conversations with AI, hundreds of hours bouncing around what I actually wanted to build. And one thread kept coming back: skilled trades. The physical, mechanical, problem-solving work that has a feedback loop you can touch. Either the panel works or it doesn't. Nobody can deck-ify that.

Looking back, the thread connecting all of this is less a philosophy and more a habit: I need to open the box and see the wiring before I'll trust it. The math minor wasn't about becoming a mathematician. It was about not getting fooled again by a narrative. The trades interest isn't romanticism. It's that the work is honest in a way that most of what I'd been doing wasn't. And the people I've actually respected in the workplace weren't the ones with the cleanest quarterly story. They were the ones who could sit in a long, quiet, unproductive stretch without reaching for a distraction. Something still gets built in that silence. Something never gets built in the noise.
