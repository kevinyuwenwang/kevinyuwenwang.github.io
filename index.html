About

Early Years

Middle School

Shanghai. Restless, chronically in the principal's office—either for disruption or for the side hustles I was running. At one point I was selling jailbroken iPhone apps to classmates and making more money than a 13-year-old should. At 15 my parents shipped me to a small academy in the Denver suburbs for a change of scenery.

High School

An overweight Chinese kid who learned English from textbooks, dropped into a school where everyone had known each other since kindergarten. First year: confused, homesick. Junior year, something shifted. I wanted to prove I could reshape myself through sustained effort. Took up basketball and soccer with zero natural talent. Hours of running after school, a steady diet of Jimmy John's, and I lost 30 kilograms in a year. The discipline bled into academics. I started paying attention, ran for student government treasurer, and picked up an interest in finance.

College

At 18 I opened my first brokerage account and discovered how fast money gets made and lost. Started with dividends—Macy's stock, just to get paid for holding. Got bored, switched to Bitcoin. Then binged YouTube trading gurus, wasting entire days hunting chart patterns that didn't exist.

Then leverage. 10x, 20x—felt like printing money. Lasted until a whipsaw wiped the account on a random Tuesday. Margin call received mid-itinerary on a packed travel day. Skipped the hotel, slept in the car. A few months later I tried options. 100x leverage imploded faster.

The cycle repeated until a junior-year afternoon when I found Optimal Mean Reversion Trading. The equations looked like a foreign language, but the intuition was clear: this tier of math separated traders from gamblers. My foundation was too thin for what I was attempting, so I added a mathematics minor. My parents thought I'd lost my mind.

Graduate School

Watching classmates compete for the same saturated finance internships made me reconsider the default path. I've always thought better alone than at career fairs. The arrival of LLMs changed the calculus. Months of late-night conversations with AI—hundreds of hours of rambling about where to point my career—and one thread surfaced: skilled trades.

Principles

Happiness Is Internal

Most people build lives around money, status, experiences, then wonder why satisfaction fades. You hit the target, feel the brief high, revert to baseline—hedonic adaptation. When boredom or sadness creeps in, the default response is to reach for another external reward, which only generates more friction.

The fix isn't a bigger reward. It's training the ability to sit with silence, boredom, and discomfort without reaching for a distraction. Happiness is generated internally. Chasing external fixes keeps you on a treadmill.

Ground in Objective Reality

My single test for whether a person is good: do they lie to themselves?

People who lie to themselves operate on the laws of their own delusions, selectively disregarding how the world actually works. The antidote is studying disciplines that don't negotiate: mathematics, physics, engineering. Thermodynamics, conservation of energy, the law of large numbers—these don't care what you want to be true. Aligning with them strips away the ability to self-deceive and reveals your actual position inside a system.

Seek Asymmetric Value

My trading years taught me that wealth creation is explosive, not linear. It defies the corporate default of trading a fixed block of labor for a fixed wage. A hundred men cannot push what one engine moves.

A single correct mental framework outweighs years of incremental busyness. So I strip life to its components—food, shelter, basic security—optimize them efficiently, and preserve the remaining mental bandwidth for deep, compounding thought. Free from office politics and the labor of social signaling.

Talent Requires Judgment

"Why not just hire smart people to do the thinking?" Because talent only compounds under competent, truthful judgment.

A leader who lacks grounding in reality cannot tell a builder from a performer. The performer optimizes quarterly KPIs while quietly eroding long-term value. The builder posts worse short-term numbers because they're actually investing in R&D. If the leader rewards the performer, the system eats itself: good workers leave unrecognized, bad ones thrive. A leader with real judgment attracts people who stay for the mission, even for less pay. That kind of cognitive ability and calibration is rare. Money can't buy it.
