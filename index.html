The Story

Growing up in Shanghai, I was the kid perpetually in the principal's office—half for disruption, half for whatever side hustle I was running. I was selling jailbroken iPhone apps to classmates and making more than most 13-year-olds earn in a year. At 15 my parents decided I needed a different zip code and shipped me to a small academy in the Denver suburbs.

Landing in a school where everyone had known each other since kindergarten, as a kid who'd learned English from textbooks, was its own kind of shock. First year I was mostly lost. Junior year I got it in my head that I could reshape myself through sustained effort, nothing more. Picked up basketball and soccer with no natural talent to speak of. Ran for hours after school, ate a steady diet of Jimmy John's, lost 30 kilograms in a year. That discipline bled into the classroom. I started actually listening, ran for student government treasurer, and found an interest in finance.

College is where I learned how fast money moves. At 18 I opened a brokerage account and went through the motions—dividends first, Macy's stock, just to feel like I was getting paid for holding. Then Bitcoin, because I got impatient. Then a month of YouTube gurus and hours spent hunting chart patterns that weren't there.

Then leverage. 10x, 20x. Felt effortless. Lasted until a random Tuesday whipsaw wiped the account. Got the margin call mid-trip, on a packed itinerary. Didn't go to the hotel. Slept in the car. A few months later I tried options. 100x leverage. Gone faster.

The cycle kept repeating until a junior-year afternoon when I picked up Optimal Mean Reversion Trading. The equations were a foreign language, but the signal was clear: this level of math is what separates a trader from a gambler. My foundation was too thin for what I was attempting, so I added a mathematics minor. My parents thought I'd lost my mind.

Graduate school made the career question unavoidable. Watching classmates grind for the same saturated finance internships, and knowing I'd always thought better alone than at a networking event, I started looking sideways. Then LLMs arrived and changed what was possible. Months of late-night conversations with AI, hundreds of hours bouncing around career paths and industry structure. Somewhere around the hundredth hour of that process, one thread kept surfacing: skilled trades.

How I Think

The first and most important rule I hold myself to: don't lie to yourself. When something doesn't work, look at what actually happened. People who avoid that step end up operating on the physics of their own delusions. I lean heavily on disciplines that don't negotiate—math, physics, engineering—because thermodynamics and the law of large numbers don't care what narrative you want to maintain. Studying them is how I stay honest about where I actually stand inside a system.

That honesty has a practical payoff: I don't need the next external reward to keep going. Most people run on a cycle—hit the target, feel the high, revert to baseline, reach for the next target. I'd rather train the ability to sit in silence and boredom without reaching for a distraction. The treadmill and the path look similar from the outside. The difference is whether you stop when the reward stops.

On execution, I've stopped believing in the linear model. My trading years made it obvious: value is created in jumps. A hundred men can't push what one engine moves. A single correct framework outweighs years of incremental busyness. So I keep life as simple as I can—food, shelter, basic security handled efficiently—and protect the remaining mental space for the kind of thinking that actually compounds. No office politics, no performing for an audience.

And when I'm building with other people, I'm looking for the same grounding I demand of myself. I can usually tell a builder from a performer. The builder will have uglier short-term numbers because they're actually investing in something durable. The performer will have a clean quarterly narrative and nothing underneath. If a leader can't tell the difference, the system eats itself: good people leave unrecognized, bad ones climb. I'd rather work with a small group who care about the actual work than a large one that costs less and produces nothing.
